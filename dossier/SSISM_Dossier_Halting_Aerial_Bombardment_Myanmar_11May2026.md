# SSISM Strategic Dossier

## Halting Aerial Bombardment in Myanmar  
### Legal, Strategic, and Operational Pathways

**Date:** 11 May 2026  
**Classification:** Strategic Policy Analysis / SSISM Framework

---

## Abstract

Across Myanmar, the most urgent civilian appeal is simple: **stop the bombing from the sky**.

Air strikes conducted since 2021 have become one of the most devastating aspects of the conflict, with repeated allegations of impacts on villages, medical sites, schools, religious compounds, and displacement areas.

While proposals such as a UN-mandated No-Fly Zone face geopolitical barriers, **practical, lawful, non-military pathways exist** to reduce and potentially halt aerial bombardment. These pathways rely on supply-chain control, financial leverage, recognition policy, and accountability mechanisms already available to states, regional bodies, companies, and civil society.

This dossier consolidates those pathways into an actionable, law-aligned framework.

---

## 1. The Legal Framework Governing Air Strikes

Myanmar is widely assessed as being in a non-international armed conflict (NIAC).

Applicable rules arise from:

- Common Article 3 of the Geneva Conventions
- Customary International Humanitarian Law
- Norms reflected in Additional Protocol II
- Standards reflected in the Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court

Four key obligations govern aerial operations:

1. **Distinction** – Only military objectives may be targeted.  
2. **Proportionality** – Civilian harm must not be excessive.  
3. **Precautions** – Feasible steps to verify targets and reduce harm are required.  
4. **Protection of civilian objects** – Hospitals, schools, religious sites, and IDP camps receive special protection.

Documented strike patterns raise serious concerns under these principles.

---

## 2. Why “No-Fly Zone” Is Not the Immediate Lever

A traditional No-Fly Zone requires:

- Combat air patrol enforcement
- Regional basing permissions
- Willingness to neutralize radar and air defenses
- Major power military commitment

Given geopolitical realities, this pathway is unlikely in the near term.

**The workable alternative is not controlling the sky.  
It is denying the ground infrastructure that makes flight possible.**

---

## 3. Aircraft Depend on Civilian Supply Chains

All aircraft operations depend on:

- Jet fuel (Jet A-1 or equivalents)
- Continuous maintenance and replacement parts
- Financial channels for procurement
- International insurance, shipping, and port access

These are civilian and commercial systems, subject to lawful regulation and denial without any UN Security Council mandate.

---

## 4. High-Impact Non-Military Measures Available Now

### A. Aviation Fuel Interdiction

- Classify aviation fuel exports as high-risk dual-use goods
- Require end-use verification for shipments
- Inspect, delay, or deny suspect cargoes
- Sanction shipping brokers and insurers involved

Even partial disruption reduces sortie frequency.

---

### B. Maintenance and Spare Parts Embargo

- Identify and blacklist intermediary suppliers
- Restrict servicing and software support via third countries
- Sanction maintenance networks

Operational reliability degrades over time.

---

### C. Financial Channel Restrictions

- Target banks facilitating aviation procurement
- Freeze accounts linked to supply networks
- Apply secondary sanctions where appropriate

---

### D. Airspace and Logistics Denial

- Deny overflight or landing permissions for military-linked aircraft
- Increase scrutiny of dual-use cargo flights
- Tighten airport fuel monitoring

---

## 5. Recognition Policy as Operational Leverage

Political recognition affects practical procurement ability.

Shifting recognition patterns toward democratic actors:

- Raises legal and reputational risk for suppliers
- Weakens claims of sovereign legitimacy for procurement
- Enables stricter enforcement without new sanctions

Recognition becomes a non-military operational lever.

---

## 6. Accountability as Deterrence

Through universal jurisdiction principles and Rome Statute standards:

- Documentation of strikes
- Identification of command responsibility
- Preparation of legal case files

This increases personal risk for those ordering or executing unlawful attacks.

---

## 7. Humanitarian and Civilian Protection Measures

- Early warning networks for air activity
- Cross-border humanitarian corridors
- Community shelter and dispersion strategies

---

## 8. Stakeholder Action Matrix

| Stakeholder | Practical Measures |
|---|---|
| ASEAN member states | Port inspections, no normalization without benchmarks, humanitarian access outside junta channels |
| Like-minded governments | Sanctions on fuel/parts networks, financial restrictions, support for documentation |
| Civil society & NGOs | Evidence gathering, supplier exposure, advocacy for fuel embargo enforcement |
| Legal networks | Universal jurisdiction case preparation |
| Aviation & shipping industry | Enhanced due diligence, refusal of high-risk consignments |

---

## 9. Monitoring Indicators (Next 3–6 Months)

- Fuel shortages or rising aviation fuel prices inside Myanmar
- Reduced air sortie frequency
- Sanctions on aviation supply intermediaries
- Diplomatic language shifting away from normalization
- Expansion of cross-border aid mechanisms

---

## Conclusion

Stopping aerial bombardment does not require controlling Myanmar’s airspace.

It requires controlling the **fuel, parts, finance, logistics, and legitimacy** that make those flights possible.

These levers are already available, lawful, and non-military.

---

### Executive Summary (One Sentence)

**Coordinated disruption of aviation fuel, maintenance supply chains, financial access, and political recognition offers the most feasible path to reducing and potentially halting air strikes in Myanmar.**
