Monetary Dependency, Digital Infrastructure, and Financial Extraction in Myanmar (2021–2026)

Abstract

This paper examines the transformation of Myanmar's economic landscape between 2021 and 2026 through the lens of economic sovereignty, conflict economics, and digital dependency. Drawing upon publicly available economic assessments, policy developments, and financial governance trends, the study investigates three critical dynamics:

1. The strategic role of migrant-worker remittances.
2. Growing monetary integration with regional financial systems.
3. Expansion of foreign-controlled digital and payment infrastructure.

The paper argues that prolonged conflict has elevated financial control into a primary instrument of institutional survival. As traditional economic capacity deteriorates, competition increasingly centers on currency access, digital payment architecture, and external financial relationships. These developments raise significant questions regarding long-term economic autonomy and national resilience.

Keywords: Myanmar, Economic Sovereignty, Conflict Economics, Digital Payments, Political Economy, Remittances, Financial Dependency, Southeast Asia

1. Introduction

Economic crises generated by conflict rarely remain confined to battlefield outcomes.

In contemporary states, monetary systems, payment infrastructure, digital platforms, and financial networks form part of the strategic environment. The capacity to regulate and control these systems increasingly determines institutional resilience.

Myanmar presents a significant contemporary case study. Since 2021, the country has experienced persistent currency instability, declining investment confidence, weakened productive sectors, and expanding informal economic activity.

This paper explores how these pressures have altered the relationship between sovereignty, finance, and technology.

---

2. Economic Sovereignty: A Conceptual Framework

Economic sovereignty refers to the ability of a state to independently exercise authority over:

- Monetary policy
- Currency management
- Financial regulation
- Strategic economic infrastructure
- Resource allocation

In periods of stability these mechanisms operate under domestic authority.

Under conditions of conflict, however, fiscal pressures often create dependency upon external actors, foreign currency inflows, and alternative financing arrangements.

The result can be a gradual erosion of policy autonomy despite short-term gains in financial stability.

---

3. Remittances and the Political Economy of Survival

Remittance flows constitute one of the most important sources of foreign currency entering Myanmar.

Historically these funds supported:

- Household consumption
- Education
- Healthcare
- Housing investment

Under current economic conditions, however, remittances carry broader macroeconomic significance.

Foreign currency inflows support:

- Liquidity management
- Import financing
- Exchange-rate stabilization
- Balance-of-payments sustainability

Consequently, migrant-worker earnings have evolved from a household welfare mechanism into a strategic component of national financial resilience.

---

4. Monetary Integration and External Financial Influence

Regional financial integration has accelerated across Asia through:

- Digital payment systems
- Cross-border settlements
- Electronic currencies
- Fintech partnerships

These arrangements can increase efficiency and facilitate trade.

However, dependency risks emerge when a smaller economy becomes structurally reliant upon the monetary and technological infrastructure of larger external partners.

Questions therefore arise regarding:

- Regulatory independence
- Monetary autonomy
- Financial transparency
- Strategic leverage

Myanmar's evolving financial relationships provide an important case through which to examine these dynamics.

---

5. Digital Infrastructure as Strategic Economic Power

The twenty-first century economy increasingly depends upon digital infrastructure.

Historically strategic assets included:

- Ports
- Railways
- Energy networks
- Industrial zones

Today strategic influence is also exercised through:

- Cloud computing platforms
- Payment gateways
- Financial databases
- Data networks

Control over these systems shapes the flow of information, capital, and economic activity.

As digital infrastructure becomes essential to everyday commerce, questions of digital sovereignty become inseparable from broader economic sovereignty.

---

6. Conflict Economics and Institutional Adaptation

Conflict environments generate adaptive economic behavior.

Common mechanisms include:

- Currency controls
- Informal trade systems
- Resource extraction
- External partnerships
- Alternative taxation structures

While these mechanisms may enhance short-term survival, they can also produce long-term structural vulnerabilities.

Myanmar illustrates this dilemma.

Policies designed to preserve immediate fiscal capacity may simultaneously deepen future dependency upon external actors, technologies, or financial networks.

---

7. Implications for Myanmar's Future

Several observations emerge from this assessment.

First, economic sovereignty increasingly depends upon control of digital infrastructure.

Second, foreign currency access remains central to institutional resilience.

Third, financial partnerships can simultaneously provide support and generate dependency.

Fourth, future reconstruction efforts must consider not only physical infrastructure but also financial governance and digital autonomy.

The architecture of payment systems may prove as important as roads, ports, and energy projects in shaping Myanmar's future development trajectory.

---

8. Conclusion

Myanmar's ongoing crisis demonstrates that modern political struggles extend beyond conventional military arenas.

Financial systems, digital infrastructure, and monetary governance have become strategic domains in their own right.

The interaction between remittance flows, monetary integration, and digital dependency highlights the evolving relationship between sovereignty and economic survival in the twenty-first century.

For scholars of political economy, Myanmar offers an important case study in how conflict reshapes the institutions that underpin economic autonomy.
